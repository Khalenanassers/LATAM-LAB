Which invoices will be paid late? A logistic regression, explained

We trained a logistic regression (a simple model that adds up clues about an invoice) on past VIVA invoices, then tested it on other invoices it had never seen. For each one it guessed: will the customer pay more than 30 days late? Then we checked every guess against what really happened. The settings used in the code are explained in section 3.

What do the numbers mean? (glossary)

Accuracy: out of all invoices, how many guesses were correct.
Recall: of the invoices that really were late, how many we caught. Low recall = late payers slip through.
Precision: when the model says “late”, how often it is right. Low precision = wasted calls.
F1: one score blending recall and precision.
AUC: how well the model ranks risky invoices above safe ones (0.5 = coin flip, 1.0 = perfect).
Odds ratio: how much the odds of “late” are multiplied when a clue goes up by one standard step. 1 = no effect, above 1 = more late, below 1 = less late.
Stratified: the split keeps the same share of late invoices in both the training and test groups.
Regularization: a penalty that stops the model from leaning too hard on any one clue.

Things to know before trusting this

  • The test is small ( invoices, only late). A few invoices more or less would move every percentage.
  • The model leans heavily on the customer's risk tier, which is also how the practice data was built, so real-world results would likely be lower.
  • The three tier clues always add to 1 (every customer is in exactly one tier), so they overlap. The regularization penalty keeps the model stable, but read the tier weights as a group, not one by one.
  • The invoices are split at random, so the model sometimes learns from invoices newer than the ones it is tested on. Section 5 shows what happens on a strict past-to-future split.
  • Seed 2995 is fixed, so rerunning gives identical results.
